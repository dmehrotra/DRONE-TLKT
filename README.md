# The Age of the Drone Police Is Here

**By Dhruv Mehrotra & Jesse Marx**
*June 5, 2024*

On a Wednesday afternoon in August, Daniel Posada and his girlfriend were screaming at each other at a bus stop when someone called 911. From a rooftop a mile away, the Chula Vista Police Department started the rotors of a 13-pound drone.

The drone, a Matrice 300 RTK, lifted into the air equipped with a high-resolution camera, thermal imaging, and a zoom lens. It streamed footage to a precinct officer, the department’s Real-Time Operations Center, and the officer en route.

It flew 392 feet above Chula Vista, a San Diego suburb, passing near sensitive sites including a preschool and church, potentially exposing thousands to surveillance unrelated to the 911 call.

Posada, known as “Focal” in the local homeless community, heard the drone above him just before a police car stopped him. He was searched. No violence had occurred, and the argument hadn’t escalated. Posada questioned the response, feeling targeted by a system that prioritizes surveillance over social services.

> “I could understand sending a drone for something serious,” he says. “I feel like a target.”

## Chula Vista: Drone First Responder Pioneer

The Chula Vista Police Department (CVPD) was the first in the U.S. to launch a *Drone as First Responder (DFR)* program in 2018. Listening to 911 calls, teleoperators decide in real-time when to deploy drones. Since then, the drones have been dispatched nearly 20,000 times for incidents ranging from car accidents and overdoses to noise complaints and homicides.

The CVPD claims that drones reduce unnecessary police contact, improve response times, and save lives. However, WIRED’s analysis reveals complex trade-offs between public safety and civil liberties.

Drone flight paths reflect socioeconomic divides. WIRED’s review of nearly 10,000 drone flight records from 2021–2023 shows disproportionate drone exposure in Chula Vista’s poorer, more immigrant-heavy west side. Drones are used not only for serious incidents but also for minor ones like shoplifting and loud music. At the start of the pandemic, drones were even used to broadcast public announcements to homeless camps.

Residents report feeling under constant surveillance. Some avoid spending time outdoors or near windows. One individual said the stress led him to the ER for depression.

## Surveillance Patterns and Privacy Concerns

The drones, capable of capturing facial details, have filmed hundreds of hours of footage while flying over sensitive sites—hospitals, schools, churches, law offices, and more. Privacy advocates question whether this constitutes mass surveillance. The department states that drones are only dispatched in response to 911 calls or lawful searches, and flight logs generally support that—though some 10% of flights lacked a clear stated purpose.

Some residents expressed trust in the program, while others cited mission creep—where the presence of drones justifies expanded use. Several ex-CVPD leaders now work for drone manufacturers, promoting the model elsewhere.

## Methods

WIRED analyzed over 22 million drone flight coordinates. Each flight passed over an average of 13 census blocks, exposing roughly 4,700 people to drone surveillance. The data showed that poorer neighborhoods received more drone exposure, especially the working-class west side, compared to wealthier, less-patrolled areas in the east.

CVPD argues the discrepancy reflects higher crime reports in those areas. Dispatch logs support this, but critics point to systemic over-policing in marginalized communities.

Residents on the east side, where incomes are higher and privacy expectations stronger, reported fewer drone sightings and expressed more abstract concerns. Meanwhile, west side residents shared stories of constant drone presence and invasion of privacy.
